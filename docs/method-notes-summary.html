Method notes in brief

The short version: what the tool computes, and — the part that matters most when you cite it — what it does not model at all. Two independent engines are provided, and the disagreement between them is itself a teaching device. The full method notes give the measurements, the source values and the references behind every statement here.

Four things to know before you cite a number

  1. The obstruction angle is read by the split-flux engine only. Set it while the raytraced engine is selected and nothing changes — that engine has no external obstruction of any kind in its scene.
  2. Every surface reflects diffusely. There is no specular reflection anywhere, so a polished light shelf, a mirrored louvre or a glossy floor will be under-predicted.
  3. sDA and ASE here are not LM-83 results. LM-83 requires modelled blind operation; this tool has no blinds and no shading schedule.
  4. Design sky illuminance does nothing under CIE Clear or CIE Intermediate. Those two skies take their absolute levels from the tool's own clear-sky model, and the setting is overwritten. It governs CIE Overcast and Uniform only.

What it calculates

Six metrics. Two are instantaneous, computed for the single date and time on the scrubber — illuminance in lux and daylight factor as a percentage. Four are annual, computed over the occupied hours of a whole year from one pass: useful daylight illuminance, daylight autonomy (and sDA from it), annual sunlight exposure, and direct sun hours.

The raytraced engine works by daylight coefficients over Tregenza/Reinhart sky patches, with one extra column for the ground. The split-flux engine is the BRE hand method — sky component, externally reflected component and internally reflected component, added. The statistics panel also reports the BS 8206-2 average daylight factor, which is a genuine hand-calculable cross-check on the grid mean.

A detail worth knowing before you quote a UDI figure. Hours when the sun is below the horizon still count as occupied hours, and they fall into the lowest bin. An 08:00–18:00 occupancy in a London winter therefore carries genuinely dark hours in the denominator.

Not modelled at all

Context geometry — no surrounding buildings, no trees, no terrain, no courtyard walls. The scene contains the room and nothing else. Specular reflection — every surface is Lambertian, so light-shelf studies in particular will mislead. Directional glazing — transmittance is diffuse-equivalent and constant with angle, so no Fresnel, no BSDF, no prismatic glazing. Blinds and controls — no shading schedule, no electric lighting, no daylight-linked dimming. Interior content — no furniture, no partitions; the workplane is a transparent sensor plane that neither blocks nor bounces light. Colour and glare — grey and photopic throughout, and no DGP, DGI or any luminance-based glare metric.

There is no ground plane in the raytraced scene, so the building casts no shadow on its own ground in the calculation. The shadows in the 3D view are renderer shadows and play no part in the numbers.

How exact the numbers are

Measured by the tool's own audit, which drives the shipped file in headless Chromium and compares against results derived independently of the code. An unobstructed point reads 100.0017% against an exact 100%; rectangular roof apertures match closed-form configuration factors to ±0.12%. What the browser approximations cost:

up to −20%
Bounce truncation
The most significant approximation. In a deliberately bright room, going from 4 to 12 bounces raises mean DF by 20%. Shipped tiers use 1–5, so rooms with very light surfaces read low. Above ρ ≈ 0.8, use the Fine tier or read the result as a lower bound.
±0.005
Monte-Carlo noise
On the mean DF at Standard quality. Re-running the same model will not give a bit-identical answer, so quote to one decimal place, not three.
−1.1%
One sun-disc sample per annual hour
A binary shadow with no penumbra. This is the accuracy floor for ASE and sun hours near a shadow edge.
1.6%
Grid symmetry
Mean left–right discrepancy on a symmetric room. This is the noise floor, not a bug — it is why you read the field, not individual points.

The default 0.5 m grid spacing is comfortably grid-independent for an ordinary room: mean DF moves from 9.362% at 1 m to 9.347% at 0.5 m to 9.231% at 0.25 m.

Against Radiance and DIALux

Provenance. Everything else here is read from the source code. This section is not, and cannot be: the code says nothing about how Radiance or DIALux work. Check it against their documentation before relying on it.

Should agree on method family (daylight coefficients over Tregenza/Reinhart patches, as in Radiance's rcontrib), on solar geometry, on simple diffuse cases, and on the hand method — the split-flux ADF is the BS 8206-2 calculation, coefficient table and all.

Will diverge on anything urban (this scene contains only the room), on specular surfaces, on complex fenestration, and in very light rooms, where Radiance is typically run at more bounces than the shipped tiers allow.

Three exercises

Each isolates one variable and holds everything else fixed. Run all three on the Raytraced engine at Standard quality unless told otherwise, and let the calculation settle before reading the statistics panel.

Exercise 01

Window head height, not window area, sets how deep daylight goes

  1. Room L 6.0 · W 12.0 · H 3.6 m, grid spacing 0.25 m, workplane 0.75 m. Disable every opening except one south window, set to w 5.0 · h 1.5 · sill 0.9 — a head height of 2.4 m.
  2. Sky CIE Overcast, metric DF. Note the distance from the window at which DF falls below 2%, and record the mean and the min:mean uniformity.
  3. Now change only the sill, to 1.9 m. Height and width are unchanged, so the glazed area is identical — but the head is now 3.4 m. Re-read all three.

Principle. Daylight penetration depth scales with the height of the window head above the workplane, not with glazed area. Because you changed nothing else, any difference is attributable to head height alone. This is the geometric basis of the rule of thumb that useful daylight reaches roughly 1.5–2× the head height into a side-lit room.

Exercise 02

A shading device that lowers the daylight factor can improve the daylight

  1. Reset to the default studio classroom, London, with all its openings. Metric DF: record the mean and the percentage of area above 2%.
  2. Switch to UDI, thresholds 150 / 300 / 500 / 3000, occupied hours 08–18. Record the Too high mean, then the Useful — in range + high mean.
  3. Add a horizontal overhang to the south window only: depth 0.6 · count 1 · offset 0.10 · extend 0.4 · tilt 0. Re-read all four numbers.
  4. Finally, switch the engine to Split-flux and back, comparing the DF mean each time.

Principle. DF has no upper bound and cannot express “too much light”, so it falls whenever you shade. UDI has an upper bound, so shading can move hours out of the over-lit bin and into the useful one. The two metrics can move in opposite directions for the same design change, and knowing which question you are asking decides which one to quote. Step 4 adds a second lesson: on a simple overhang the two engines sit about 3% apart, but on a five-blade louvre bank the audit measures 12% — split-flux degrades as shading structure gets finer.

Exercise 03

Why an overhang works on the south facade and a fin is needed on the east

  1. Default studio classroom, London. Metric Direct sun hours; wait for the annual run. Record mean and maximum, then switch to ASE (1000 lux / 250 h) and record the percentage of area.
  2. Set Project north to 90° — the same room, rotated so the large opening faces east. Re-run and record both figures again.
  3. Return north to 0°. Add a horizontal overhang of depth 0.8 to the east window and note how little ASE changes. Remove it and add vertical fins instead: depth 0.4 · count 4 · side both. Re-read ASE.
  4. Turn on Solar rays and scrub the time slider through a summer morning to see why.

Principle. Solar altitude, not orientation alone, decides which device works. South sun at midday is high, so a shallow horizontal projection cuts it. East and west sun arrives low and nearly normal to the glass, where a horizontal overhang of any reasonable depth barely intersects it and only vertical elements help. The ray view makes the geometry visible rather than asserted.

A caution for all three: quote differences, not absolute values. Monte-Carlo noise at Standard quality is around ±0.005 on the mean DF, so a change smaller than about 0.1 percentage points is not a result.

Where it stops

For a compliance submission, use IESVE, Radiance via Ladybug and Honeybee, or another validated tool. This one is built to teach relationships and orders of magnitude. Everything on this page is extracted from the tool's source and from its engine validation report, which is regenerated by an automated audit driving the shipped file in headless Chromium. Where a figure is quoted it is measured, not estimated.

References

What the sky models, the solar algorithm and the metrics are drawn from. These are the sources the code itself names; where it names none — the glazing and reflectance presets among them — none is listed. The code names each source but not its bibliographic detail, so that was added from the published record.

Sky models

CIE S 011/E:2003 (= ISO 15469:2004). Spatial Distribution of Daylight — CIE Standard General Sky. Vienna. — All fifteen types are in the code, four are offered in the interface, and type 1, the overcast sky, is the basis of every daylight factor reported.

Perez, R., Seals, R. & Michalsky, J. (1993). ‘All-weather model for sky luminance distribution’, Solar Energy, 50(3), pp. 235–245. — The all-weather sky, used whenever a climate file drives the calculation.

Daylight coefficients

Tregenza, P. R. & Waters, I. M. (1983). ‘Daylight coefficients’, Lighting Research & Technology, 15(2), pp. 65–71. — The method the whole raytraced engine rests on.

Tregenza, P. R. (1987). ‘Subdivision of the sky hemisphere for luminance measurements’, Lighting Research & Technology, 19(1), pp. 13–14. — The 145-patch subdivision, a separate contribution from the 1983 paper.

Reinhart, C. F. & Walkenhorst, O. (2001). Energy and Buildings, 33(7), pp. 683–697; and Bourgeois, D., Reinhart, C. F. & Ward, G. (2008). Building Research & Information, 36(1), pp. 68–82. — The DAYSIM workflow the engine follows, and where the MF:n generalisation was formalised.

Solar position

NOAA Solar Calculator, after Meeus, J. (1998). Astronomical Algorithms, 2nd edn. Willmann-Bell; and Kasten, F. & Young, A. T. (1989). Applied Optics, 28(22), pp. 4735–4738. — Solar position, checked against published values for four cities and agreeing to 0.1°; and relative optical air mass.

The metrics

Nabil, A. & Mardaljevic, J. (2005). ‘Useful daylight illuminance’, Lighting Research & Technology, 37(1), pp. 41–59. — UDI. The default five-interval scheme extends their original 100–2000 lux proposal, which the tool also offers as a preset.

IES LM-83-12 and LEED v4 (EQ Credit: Daylight). — sDA and ASE, and the credit thresholds quoted in the interface. The tool omits the blind-operation schedule LM-83 requires, which is why both metrics are described throughout as indicative rather than as submissions. LM-83-23 has since revised the method.

The hand method

BS 8206-2:2008, Code of practice for daylighting. BSI. Withdrawn, superseded by BS EN 17037:2018+A1:2021. — The split-flux method and the average daylight factor. Cite it as a withdrawn standard, and check BS EN 17037 for current practice.

BRE (1986). Estimating daylight in buildings: Part 2 (Digest 310). — The C coefficient table for the internally reflected component. The tool extends the published 0–80° table to 90° with a constant 5, and scales the IRC by the shading devices; both are the tool's own extensions, not part of the method. Originally derived in Hopkinson, Longmore & Petherbridge (1954).

Named, but not cited

Two entries that are deliberately not citations. Perez et al. (1990) is named in the code but not implemented — a fitted luminous-efficacy curve stands in for it. And no ASHRAE publication is cited: the code calls its fallback “ASHRAE-style”, but the formula extinguishes the beam through a Linke turbidity factor, which the ASHRAE clear-sky model does not use. Treat the synthetic year as the tool's own construction — deterministic and reproducible, but not a real climate and not a standard one.
