Method & limitations

Daylight Lab Method Notes

What this tool computes, how it computes it, and — the part that matters most when you cite it — what it does not model at all. Everything below is read from the source code and the tool's own measured validation run, not from what a daylight simulator is generally assumed to do.

Audience BSc / MSc architecture Engines Raytraced daylight coefficients · Split-flux (BRE) Runs Entirely in the browser

Three things to know before you cite a number

  1. The obstruction angle is read by the split-flux engine only. Set it while the raytraced engine is selected and nothing changes — the raytracer has no external obstruction of any kind in its scene.
  2. Every surface reflects diffusely. There is no specular reflection anywhere, so a polished light shelf, a mirrored louvre or a glossy floor will be under-predicted.
  3. sDA and ASE here are not LM-83 results. LM-83 requires modelled blind operation; this tool has no blinds and no shading schedule.
01

What it calculates

Six metrics. Two are instantaneous — computed for the single date and time on the scrubber — and four are annual, computed over the occupied hours of a whole year.

Illuminance Elux0One instant

Useful Daylight Illuminance UDI% of hours0Annual, 5 intervals
Daylight Autonomy DA% of hours0Annual

Direct sun hourshours0Annual, geometric

Illuminance

The sum of a diffuse and a direct term. In the raytraced engine the diffuse term is a dot product between the point's daylight-coefficient row and the sky-patch luminance vector, with one extra column for the ground:

E_diffuse(p) = Σᵢ DC[p][i] · Lᵢ + DC[p][ground] · L_ground lux

The direct term samples the sun disc and multiplies by the product of glazing transmittances along the ray — four disc samples when the model is at rest, one while a slider is being dragged:

E_direct(p) = E_normal · (1/K) Σₖ cos θₖ · τ_path(k)

Daylight Factor

Computed as a ratio against the diffuse horizontal illuminance of the same CIE overcast sky. The direct beam is explicitly switched off for this pass, so DF never contains sunlight. The design sky illuminance cancels out of the ratio.

DF(p) = 100 · E_overcast(p) / E_diffuse-horizontal

Under the split-flux engine, DF is instead the classic BRE hand method — three components added, the first two varying point by point and the third a single room-wide number:

DF = SC + ERC + IRC SC numerical integration over each aperture, 8×8 samples (16×16 when any shading device is present), CIE overcast relative luminance (1 + 2 sin α)/3, normalised by 7π/9 ERC directions below the obstruction angle credited at the ground reflectance; directions blocked by the building's own shading devices credited at half the device reflectance IRC = (T·W / (A(1−ρ))) · (C·ρ_fw + 5(ρ_cw − ρ_fw)) C interpolated from the BRE table [39, 35, 31, 25, 20, 14, 10, 7, 5, 5, 5] in 10° steps

One deliberate departure from the textbook: the BRE internally reflected component contains no shading term, so on its own it would not respond to an overhang at all. The tool scales IRC by the same fraction of sky flux the devices remove. This is an extension of BS 8206-2, not part of it, and the tool's own Engine panel says so.

The statistics panel also reports the BS 8206-2 average daylight factor, ADF = T·W·θ / (A(1 − ρ²)), with θ = 90° − obstruction angle. This is a genuine hand-calculable cross-check on the grid mean.

The four annual metrics

All four come from one pass over the year. For each occupied hour the engine builds a Perez sky from that hour's irradiance, computes diffuse illuminance at every point, adds the direct beam, and accumulates:

MetricAccumulated as
UDIHour counted into one of five bins by total illuminance. Displayed as a percentage of occupied hours for a selectable bin combination — the default view is “in range + high”.
DAHours where E ≥ target, as a percentage of occupied hours. sDA is then the percentage of grid points whose DA ≥ 50%.
ASEHours where the direct component alone reaches the ASE threshold and the sun is geometrically visible. The headline ASE % is the share of points exceeding the hours threshold.
Sun hoursHours where any unblocked beam reaches the point at all, whatever its magnitude.

A detail worth knowing before you quote a UDI figure: hours when the sun is below the horizon are still counted as occupied hours, and they fall into the lowest UDI bin. An 8:00–18:00 occupancy in a London winter therefore carries genuinely dark hours in the denominator.

Summary statistics reported

Minimum, maximum, mean, median, standard deviation, uniformity = min/mean, diversity = min/max, and the percentage of points at or above the metric's target.

02

The sky and sun model

Sky discretisation

The sky vault is divided into Reinhart patches — 145 at MF:1, 577 at MF:2, in both cases plus a single ground slot. Row structure at MF:1 is [30, 30, 24, 24, 18, 12, 6] with a zenith cap. Because every calculation reduces to a dot product against a patch-luminance vector, changing the sky never re-runs the raytracer — one bake serves the daylight factor, any instant, and all 8,760 hours.

Each patch carries its exact projected solid angle, (π/n)(sin²a₂ − sin²a₁), integrated over the band rather than evaluated at the patch centre. The centre approximation put a systematic −0.55% on every daylight factor and was caught by the unobstructed benchmark plateauing at 99.45%.

The CIE skies use the standard gradation and indicatrix functions, with all fifteen coefficient sets present in the code:

φ(Z) = 1 + a · exp(b / cos Z) gradation f(χ) = 1 + c(exp(dχ) − exp(dπ/2)) + e · cos²χ indicatrix L(patch) = f(χ) · φ(Z) / (f(Zs) · φ(0))

The Perez implementation carries the full eight-bin, twenty-coefficient table and includes the special case for the clearest bin exactly as Radiance's gendaylit does. Relative luminances are then scaled so the patch set integrates to the target diffuse horizontal illuminance.

Sun position

NOAA Solar Calculator algorithm (Meeus, low-precision), taking latitude, longitude, time zone, calendar date and local clock hour. It includes the equation of time, the nutation correction to apparent longitude, the obliquity correction and NOAA's piecewise atmospheric refraction fit. Sunrise, solar noon, sunset and day length use the 90.833° zenith. The tool's validation suite checks it against published values for four cities and finds agreement to 0.1°.

Project north rotates the model relative to true north; the sun vector is rotated into model space rather than the geometry being rotated.

Direct and diffuse — both, but from different machinery

Diffuse sky and ground come from the daylight-coefficient matrix. The direct beam is a separate shadow-ray calculation against the sun disc. They are added at the end. The sun's own disc is not in the patch vector, so there is no double counting.

Where the irradiance comes from

If you load an EPW, the tool reads direct normal and diffuse horizontal irradiance (columns 15 and 16) and the header's location. With no file loaded it synthesises a year from an ASHRAE-style clear-sky model — Kasten & Young air mass, Linke turbidity — modulated by a seeded random walk of cloudiness at 42% mean cover. The synthetic year is deterministic, so results are reproducible, but it is not a real climate and should never be cited as one.

Watts are converted to lumens by a simplified efficacy fit rather than the full Perez efficacy model: beam efficacy rises from about 75 lm/W at 10° solar altitude to 104 lm/W at 70°, while diffuse efficacy sits near 107 lm/W throughout.

03

What is and isn't modelled

Included

In
Interreflection
Real Monte-Carlo diffuse bounces in the raytraced engine, against each surface's own reflectance, up to the quality tier's bounce limit. Under split-flux it is the BRE average internally reflected component — one uniform number added to every point, so it cannot show a gradient.
In
Surface reflectance
Adjustable 0–0.95 for floor, walls, ceiling, external surfaces and shading devices, plus ground under Site. Reveals automatically follow the wall value. Frame (0.50) and door (0.30) are fixed and not exposed in the interface.
In
Glazing transmittance
Effective τ = glass × maintenance × frame factor, applied along the ray. Default 0.78 × 0.92 = 0.72. Seven presets from clear single (0.90) to translucent (0.20). Glass also carries ρ = 0.08 for interior reflection.
In
Frame obstruction
A real frame solid of frameWidth (default 0.05 m) around every opening in the raytraced geometry; split-flux insets its integration rectangle by the same amount. A separate frameFactor multiplier exists but is only reachable by loading a JSON model.
In
Construction thickness
Wall, roof and floor thickness generate real reveals and a real skylight well. Both shade the workplane and both are in the raytraced calculation. Glazing position through the reveal is adjustable.
In
Shading devices
Horizontal and vertical, per opening, with depth, thickness, offset, extension, blade count and tilt. Real solids in the raytraced scene; tested by occlusion in split-flux.
Partly
External obstruction
A single obstruction angle, 0–80°, read by the split-flux engine only — in the sky component, the BRE C coefficient and the ADF. The raytraced engine ignores it completely.

Not modelled

These are the assumptions a student is most likely to make wrongly, because a general-purpose daylight simulator would handle them.

Out
Context geometry
No surrounding buildings, no trees, no terrain, no courtyard walls. The scene contains the room and nothing else. The obstruction angle above is the only representation of anything outside, and only one engine reads it.
Out
Specular reflection
Every surface is Lambertian. A specular light shelf, a mirrored louvre, a glossy floor or a reflective façade opposite will all be under-predicted, and light-shelf studies in particular will mislead.
Out
Directional glazing
Transmittance is diffuse-equivalent and constant with angle. No Fresnel, no refraction, no angular τ curve, no BSDF, so prismatic glazing, films and complex fenestration cannot be represented.
Out
Blinds and controls
No blinds, no shading schedule, no electric lighting and no daylight-linked dimming. This is why sDA and ASE here are indicative rather than LM-83 submissions.
Out
Ground as a surface
The ground is a single uniform luminance, ρ · E_global / π, filling one column of the coefficient matrix. There is no ground plane in the raytraced scene, so the building casts no shadow on its own ground in the calculation. The shadows in the 3D view are renderer shadows and play no part in the numbers.
Out
Interior content
No furniture, no partitions, no occupants. The workplane itself is a transparent, non-reflecting sensor plane — it neither blocks nor bounces light, so there is no desk surface under the grid.
Out
Colour
Photopic and grey throughout. No spectral calculation, no colour bleeding between surfaces, no glazing tint colour — only its transmittance.
Out
Glare
No DGP, no DGI, no luminance-based glare metric of any kind. The “too high” UDI bin is the closest the tool comes, and it is an illuminance proxy, not a glare calculation.
Note
Calendar
The annual run uses a flat 365-day year fixed to 2001 and ignores daylight saving. 29 February in an EPW is skipped.

What the browser approximations cost

−1.1%
One sun-disc sample per annual hour
The annual loop takes a single sample, giving a binary shadow with no penumbra. Measured against 16 samples over five hours of a clear June day, mean direct illuminance differs by 1.09%. This is the accuracy floor for ASE and sun hours near a shadow edge.
up to −20%
Bounce truncation
The most significant approximation. In a deliberately bright room (walls and ceiling ρ = 0.9, floor 0.7) going from 4 to 12 bounces raises mean DF by 20%. Shipped tiers use 1–5 bounces, so rooms with very light surfaces are underestimated. Ordinary rooms at ρ ≈ 0.5 converge by 3 bounces. Above ρ ≈ 0.8, use the Fine tier or read the result as a lower bound.
±0.005
Monte-Carlo noise
Standard deviation of the mean DF across five independent bakes at Standard quality. Re-running the same model will not give a bit-identical answer; quote to one decimal place, not three.
−0.11%
Sky subdivision
MF:1 (145 patches) against MF:2 (577). Negligible for teaching purposes.
1.6%
Grid symmetry
Mean left–right discrepancy on a symmetric room; worst single point 7.7%. This is the noise floor, not a bug — it is why you read the field, not individual points.

Grid independence

Mean DF on the default model: 9.362% at 1 m spacing, 9.347% at 0.5 m, 9.231% at 0.25 m. The default 0.5 m is comfortably grid-independent for a room of this size.

06

Compared with Radiance and DIALux

Where results should agree

  • Method family. The diffuse calculation is daylight coefficients over Reinhart patches — the same approach as Radiance's rcontrib and the three-phase method. The Perez sky matches gendaylit including its special case for the clearest bin.
  • Solar geometry. NOAA/Meeus to 0.1°. No meaningful divergence from any tool.
  • Simple diffuse cases. An unobstructed point reads 100.00% DF and rectangular roof apertures match closed-form configuration factors to ±0.12%. For a plain rectangular room with matt surfaces under a CIE overcast sky, daylight factors should sit within a few per cent of a well-converged Radiance run.
  • The hand method. The split-flux ADF agrees with a BS 8206-2 hand calculation because it is that calculation, coefficient table and all.

Where results will diverge

  • Anything urban. Radiance takes an arbitrary scene; this tool's scene contains only the room. Any site with neighbours, an overshadowing wall or a courtyard will diverge completely, and no setting will close the gap.
  • Specular surfaces. Radiance and DIALux both handle specular reflection; this tool has none. Light shelves, reflective louvres and glossy finishes are the clearest failure case.
  • Complex fenestration. No BSDF, no angular transmittance. Radiance's three-phase method exists precisely for this and has no equivalent here.
  • Very light rooms. Radiance is typically run at -ab 3 to -ab 7; the Standard tier here uses 3 bounces and Fine uses 5. Above ρ ≈ 0.8 this tool reads low, by up to about 20% in the extreme case measured.
  • Shadow edges in annual runs. One sun-disc sample per hour gives hard-edged shadows, so ASE and sun-hour maps are quantised where a real penumbra would fall.
  • Compliance metrics. Without blind operation, sDA and ASE are not LM-83 results, however close the arithmetic looks.
  • DIALux specifically. DIALux is principally an electric-lighting tool built on a radiosity solver with real luminaire photometry. This tool has no electric lighting at all, so only the daylight-only case is comparable — and DIALux's radiosity approach will itself differ from a Monte-Carlo raytracer in rooms with strong interreflection.

07

Three exercises

Each isolates one variable and holds everything else fixed. Run all three on the Raytraced engine at Standard quality unless told otherwise, and let the calculation settle before reading the statistics panel.

Exercise 01

Window head height, not window area, sets how deep daylight goes

  1. Set the room to L 6.0, W 12.0, H 3.6 m. Grid spacing 0.25 m, height 0.75 m.
  2. Delete or disable every opening except one south window. Set it to w 5.0, h 1.5, sill 0.9 — a head height of 2.4 m.
  3. Sky CIE Overcast, metric DF. Note the distance from the window at which DF falls below 2%, and record the mean and the min:mean uniformity.
  4. Now change only the sill, to 1.9 m. Height stays 1.5 m, width stays 5.0 m — the glazed area is identical — but the head is now 3.4 m.
  5. Re-read the 2% depth, the mean and the uniformity.

Principle. Daylight penetration depth scales with the height of the window head above the workplane, not with glazed area. Because you changed nothing but the sill, any difference is attributable to head height alone. This is the geometric basis of the rule of thumb that useful daylight reaches roughly 1.5–2× the head height into a side-lit room.

Exercise 02

A shading device that lowers the daylight factor can improve the daylight

  1. Reset to the default studio classroom, London, with all its openings.
  2. Metric DF. Record the mean and the percentage of area above 2%.
  3. Switch to UDI, thresholds 150 / 300 / 500 / 3000, occupied hours 08–18. Set the interval view to Too high (over-lit) and record the mean. Then set it to Useful — in range + high and record that too.
  4. Add a horizontal overhang to the south window only: depth 0.6, count 1, offset 0.10, extend 0.4, tilt 0.
  5. Re-read all four numbers from steps 2 and 3.
  6. Finally, switch the engine to Split-flux and back, comparing the DF mean each time.

Principle. DF has no upper bound and cannot express “too much light”, so it falls whenever you shade. UDI has an upper bound, so shading can move hours out of the over-lit bin and into the useful one. The two metrics can move in opposite directions for the same design change, and knowing which question you are asking decides which one to quote. Step 6 adds a second lesson: on a simple overhang the two engines sit about 3% apart, but on a five-blade louvre bank the audit measures 18% — the split-flux method degrades as shading structure gets finer.

Exercise 03

Why an overhang works on the south façade and a fin is needed on the east

  1. Default studio classroom, London. Metric Direct sun hours. Wait for the annual run.
  2. Record the mean and maximum sun hours, then switch to ASE (thresholds 1000 lux / 250 h) and record the ASE percentage of area from the compliance summary.
  3. Set Project north to 90° — the same room, rotated so the large opening faces east. Re-run and record both figures again.
  4. Return project north to 0°. Add a horizontal overhang of depth 0.8 to the east window and note how little ASE changes.
  5. Remove it and add vertical fins instead: depth 0.4, count 4, side both. Re-read ASE.
  6. Turn on Solar rays and scrub the time slider through a summer morning to see why.

Principle. Solar altitude, not orientation alone, decides which device works. South sun at midday is high, so a shallow horizontal projection cuts it. East and west sun arrives low and nearly normal to the glass, where a horizontal overhang of any reasonable depth barely intersects it and only vertical elements help. The ray view in step 6 makes the geometry visible rather than asserted.

A caution for all three: quote differences, not absolute values. Monte-Carlo noise at Standard quality is around ±0.005 on the mean DF, so a change smaller than about 0.1 percentage points is not a result.
